# Load test: can the identity service take 1,000 users?

Measured on 26–27 Oct 2026 on a laptop, against the deployment in `deploy/` (Caddy with TLS in front of one
identity container, 256 MiB, external Postgres), with the container pinned to 1 or 2 vCPUs. How to rerun:
[`load/README.md`](../../load/README.md). Everything below is measured; nothing is extrapolated unless it says so.

## Short answer

**Yes, on 2 vCPUs.** 1,000 signed-in users cost almost nothing (about one token renewal a second). 1,000 people
signing in over five minutes all get in, with a sign-in taking about 70 ms. The hard case is 1,000 people pressing
"Sign in" in the same second: on 2 vCPUs everyone is in within 2½ minutes, and the median person waits 70 s and sees
"busy, try again" a dozen times first. On 1 vCPU the same burst takes about 8 minutes to drain. Nothing crashed, no
container was OOM-killed and no request was answered 500 in any run after the fix below.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| 1,000 users signed in and working (renewing a token every 15 min) | 1.1 renewals/s. The service sustains over 200/s on 1 vCPU at 5 ms median |
| All 1,000 renewing at the same moment (every tab waking up together) | All succeed within 3.4 s on 1 vCPU |
| 1,000 people signing in over 5 minutes, with renewals and key fetches beside them (2 vCPU) | All 1,000 in; sign-in p95 123 ms, renewal p95 34 ms |
| Sustained password sign-ins, 2 vCPU | About 5/s. At 4/s p95 170 ms; at 5/s some people see "busy" once (p95 3 s); at 6/s p95 140 ms |
| Sustained password sign-ins, 1 vCPU | About 2/s. At 1.5/s p95 3.9 s (2 % saw "busy" once); at 2/s p95 0.6 s |
| 1,000 people pressing "Sign in" in the same second, 2 vCPU | Everyone in within 2 min 23 s; median 70 s and 14 attempts (automatic since 27 Oct, see the follow-up). With 75 s of patience, 58–77 % get in across runs |
| The same burst on 1 vCPU | About 2 sign-ins/s drain: 23 % in within 75 s; the whole crowd would take about 8 minutes (extrapolated) |
| Memory | Peak 232 MiB of 256 MiB during a 1,000-person burst (two password checks at 64 MiB each plus 1,000 waiting connections); 150 MiB under sustained load; 83 MiB idle |

## Why sign-in is the limit, and what bounds it

A password check is argon2id with 64 MiB, 3 passes and 4 lanes (argon2-cffi defaults): 56 ms with free cores,
73 ms on 4 vCPU, 122 ms on 2, 333 ms on 1 (the 4 lanes are 4 threads, so one core serialises them). At most two
checks run at once per process (`MAX_CONCURRENT_VERIFIES`), which keeps the container under its 256 MiB; a sign-in
that cannot get a slot within 3 s (`VERIFY_QUEUE_SECONDS`) is answered **503 busy with `Retry-After: 2`**, and the
form asks the person to try again. That is the whole mechanism behind every "busy" above: the service never falls
over, it sheds.

Renewals (`POST /auth/session/token`) do no hashing, only a few indexed statements, so they are cheap: 200/s at 5 ms
on 1 vCPU; at about 240/s the backlog starts to build (p95 8 s at the 400/s stage of the ramp, every request still
answered). 1,000 users would have to renew 200 times a minute each to reach that.

## Defect found and fixed

**Sign-ins queued on the connection pool and were answered 500** (commit after this test). A 1,000-person burst on
1 vCPU produced 887 `PoolTimeout` tracebacks: each sign-in took a pooled connection for its rate-limit check *before*
waiting for a hashing slot, so 1,000 requests contended for 10 connections while the event loop was starved by the
hashes, and the ones that waited over 30 s were answered 500, a dead end for the person and a traceback in the log.
Now a sign-in takes its slot before its first database access (so at most two sign-ins ever touch the pool), a pool
timeout anywhere is answered 503 busy with `Retry-After` (JSON on the renewal endpoint, a page elsewhere), and the
wait is a setting (`DB_WAIT_SECONDS`, 30). Covered by `test_a_burst_larger_than_the_pool_never_answers_500` and
`test_a_pool_timeout_is_answered_busy`. After the fix the same burst produced 0 errors in every run.

## Open findings

1. **A shared address locks everyone out** (`nat` scenario). Failed sign-ins are counted per email *and* per client
   address, 10 per 10 minutes for both. A college network with one public address, 2 sign-ins/s and 5 % of people
   mistyping their password once hit the address limit after 70 s: from then on 233 of 360 people with the **right**
   password were refused with 429 for the rest of the run. Recommendation: a separate, much higher per-address limit
   (hundreds per 10 minutes, enough to blunt a password spray but not a classroom), or count per address+email. This
   changes a security control, so it is a decision for the owner, not made here.
2. **A burst is a bad experience even when it works.** During a 1,000-person burst the median person presses the
   button 14 times over 70 s. The form could retry on its own after `Retry-After` (the renewal endpoint's clients
   already do), or the queue wait could be longer than 3 s on a bigger host. Product decision.
3. **Memory headroom is thin in a burst**: 232 of 256 MiB. Two more concurrent checks (`MAX_CONCURRENT_VERIFIES=4`)
   would not fit. Give the host container 512 MiB if it is ever raised, or if 1,000 simultaneous connections are
   expected routinely.

## Sizing recommendation

- **2 vCPU, 512 MiB container limit (256 is enough but tight)** for a product with cohorts of up to 1,000 that may
  start a session together: sign-in bursts drain at 7–8/s, steady renewals are negligible.
- 1 vCPU is enough for steady state and for cohorts that trickle in, but a class of 100 signing in within a minute
  waits up to ~50 s at the tail.
- Keep `MAX_CONCURRENT_VERIFIES=2` on 256 MiB. Raising it needs memory before it needs cores.

## Follow-up, 27 Oct 2026: the three decisions

The review of the first run asked for three things before a college-wide rollout. All three are done and
re-measured on the same stack (2 vCPU, k6 inside the compose network).

### 1. Shared-address lockout (P0): fixed and validated

Failed sign-ins are now counted three ways, each with its own limit per 10-minute window
(`RATE_LIMIT_ATTEMPTS`, `RATE_LIMIT_ACCOUNT_ATTEMPTS`, `RATE_LIMIT_ADDRESS_ATTEMPTS`):

| Counter | Limit | What it stops | Why this number |
|---|---|---|---|
| account + client address | 10 | one person guessing at their own password | unchanged; a stranger on another address cannot trip it, so an account cannot be locked from outside (test: `failures_from_one_address_do_not_lock_the_account_elsewhere`) |
| account, from anywhere | 50 | a slow spray against one account from many addresses; caps the hashing one account can cost | 5× the personal limit: room for a household and a phone, not for a botnet |
| client address, across accounts | 500 | a password spray from one place | a college behind one public address: 1,000 students at 3.3 sign-ins/s with 10 % mistyping once is 100 failures in 5 minutes, 180 in a 10-minute window; 500 is 2.8× that, while a spray of one guess per account per 10 minutes against 500 accounts is still refused |

A successful sign-in clears the person's and the account's counters, never the address's. The verify-slot bound is the
global flood control: whatever the counters say, at most two checks run at once and the rest are answered busy.

Validation against the shared-address scenarios (one client address for everyone):

| Scenario | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 2 sign-ins/s for 3 min, 5 % mistype once | address locked after 70 s; 233 of 360 correct passwords refused | 0 refused; 360 of 360 in; p95 90 ms |
| 3.33 sign-ins/s for 5 min (1,000 people), 10 % mistype once | not run | 0 refused; 1,000 of 1,000 in; p95 307 ms |

### 2. Automatic retry (P1): done

The sign-in page now submits the form itself and, on 503, waits out `Retry-After` with a margin that grows per
attempt (up to double) and up to a second of jitter, then tries again, within a 180 s budget; the button is disabled
meanwhile and a status line says "High traffic right now. Signing you in automatically…". Only 503 is retried: a
wrong password, a refused request or a limit replaces the page with what the service answered, so a typo costs one
password check. A redirect means signed in, and the page lets the silent sign-in hand off with the same redirect and
state. Without JavaScript the form behaves as before. Checked in Chromium against a service held busy by 64 parallel
sign-ins: busy at 0.5 s, retries at 4.4, 8.3 and 12.5 s with the notice shown, hand-off with token, redirect and
state once the service was free; a wrong password showed the real page after one request, and the page retried
again after that.

The server-side picture of a burst is unchanged by design (the bound is CPU): k6 does not run the page's script, so
the burst rows above still show the manual cadence. What changes is that nobody presses anything.

### 3. Memory (P1): measured, limit raised to 512 MiB

Sampled every 2 s from inside the container (`load/memory.sh`: cgroup total, anonymous and file-backed pages, the
process's RSS and thread count):

| State | Total | Anonymous | File | RSS | Threads |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idle | 61 MiB | 50 | 6 | 79 | 5 |
| Sustained 5 sign-ins/s (mostly one check at a time) | 125 | 115 | 6 | 144 | 10 |
| 1,000-person burst, three in a row, 256 MiB limit | 232 / 242 / 232 | 219 | 6 | 248 | 15 |
| 1,000-person burst, twice, 512 MiB limit | 232 / 245 | 224 | 6 | 248 | 15 |
| 1,000 sign-ins over 5 min with renewals (final mix) | 229 | 219 | 6 | 248 | 14 |

So the budget is **50 MiB idle plus about 100 MiB per concurrent password check** (64 MiB of argon2id working memory
plus its thread and buffers), and it does not grow across bursts or with the 1,000 waiting connections (the 3.33/s
run with few connections peaks the same). Two checks peak at 232–245 MiB: 256 MiB holds, with 11–24 MiB to spare,
which is why no run was ever OOM-killed; it is also why `MAX_CONCURRENT_VERIFIES` must not be raised on 256 MiB.
Raising the limit to 512 MiB changed nothing measurable (same peaks, same sign-in rates), as expected: the bound is
CPU. `deploy/compose.yml` now sets 512 MiB, since on a 1 GB host it costs nothing and turns a 10 % margin into a
2× one; the formula above is in the file for whoever changes the slot count.

### Final verification

The mixed class-start run (1,000 sign-ins over 5 minutes at 3.33/s, renewals at 1.2/s, key fetches at 2/s; 2 vCPU,
512 MiB) after all three changes: 1,000 of 1,000 signed in, sign-in p95 97 ms and p99 164 ms, renewals 361 of 361 at
p95 28 ms, peak memory 229 MiB, no errors.

## How it was measured

- Stack: `deploy/deploy.sh --local` (the server's compose file: Caddy 2.10 with TLS, identity 256 MiB, `--cpus`
  pinned per run), Postgres 17 in `dev/docker-compose.yml`, 1,000 approved accounts seeded by `load/seed.py` with
  one argon2id hash and one live refresh session each.
- Load: k6 1.5 running **inside the compose network** against Caddy. Through Docker Desktop's port forwarding, a
  thousand simultaneous TLS connections stalled for over a minute and timed out at k6 while the service's own log
  showed it answering within seconds whenever a request reached it; those runs were discarded. The container's CPU,
  memory, restart count and OOM flag were sampled every 2 s (`load/watch.sh`).
- Two harness mistakes worth knowing about: k6 empties a VU's cookie jar after every iteration unless
  `noCookiesReset` is set, so a renewing VU replayed its old refresh token and, after the 30 s grace, the service
  revoked the session, exactly as designed, which looked like a 97 % renewal failure; and fractional arrival rates
  must be scheduled per minute, k6 only takes integers.
- Raw k6 summaries, CSV time series and container samples for every run are in the laptop's scratch folder, not in
  the repository.
